Kamuli Tourism Facilities: Hotels, Services and Travel Infrastructure in Eastern Uganda

A first-hand assessment of accommodation, transport and service development in one of Busoga's largest towns

By Mark Suer | 13 July 2026 | Based on 3 documented visits (Oct 2024 – Jan 2026)

Kamuli, one of the larger municipal centres in Uganda's Busoga sub-region, is not a town that appears on most safari itineraries. It has no national park at its doorstep, no famous crater lake, and no gorilla trekking permit office. Yet for travellers moving through eastern Uganda, Kamuli is an increasingly relevant stopover point, and for anyone interested in how tourism infrastructure develops in parts of the country that lie beyond the established circuits, it is a case study worth examining closely. Having visited the area three times between October 2024 and January 2026, I can confirm that Kamuli's tourism facilities are modest but functional, with signs of gradual improvement driven by the broader growth of Uganda's service economy.

Key Facts: Kamuli at a Glance

  • Location: Busoga sub-region, eastern Uganda, approximately 180 km northeast of Kampala
  • Status: Kamuli Municipal Council (urban municipality)
  • Poverty rate: Approximately 22%, above the national average of 16.9%
  • Primary livelihoods: Trade, transport, informal manufacturing, subsistence urban farming
  • Accommodation: Budget and mid-range guesthouses; no internationally graded luxury properties
  • Access: Road from Jinja (approx. 100 km), connected to Kampala-Jinja highway
  • Author's visits: October 2024 and January 2026 (3 visits, 3 days total)

Understanding Kamuli's Position in Uganda's Tourism Landscape

Uganda's tourism economy has grown substantially over the past decade, with the sector generating jobs, revenue, and investment across an expanding range of destinations. The country's established safari circuits — Bwindi Impenetrable National Park for gorilla trekking, Queen Elizabeth National Park for savanna game drives, Murchison Falls National Park for its dramatic waterfall and Nile cruises — absorb the majority of international visitor attention. Eastern Uganda, by contrast, has traditionally attracted fewer leisure tourists, with the notable exception of Jinja, which draws adventure seekers to the Nile's source and its world-renowned white-water rafting.

Kamuli sits in the space between Jinja and the more remote northeastern districts. It is the administrative centre of Kamuli District and functions as a municipal council with its own local government structure. The town's economy is anchored in the service sector — trade, transport, finance, education, and a nascent tourism component — alongside subsistence activities. During my first visit in October 2024, what struck me most was the contrast between the visible commercial energy of Kamuli's main trading streets and the near-total absence of anything designed with foreign visitors in mind. There were no tourist information signs, no designated viewpoints, and no accommodation properties marketing themselves to international travellers.

This is not a criticism. It reflects the reality of a town whose economic priorities are shaped by immediate needs: housing, market infrastructure, road access, and basic service delivery. Kamuli Municipal Council faces a poverty rate estimated at around 22 percent, which is above Uganda's national average of 16.9 percent. Higher poverty levels are concentrated in the rural-urban fringe zones such as Namisambya II, Nankulyaku, and parts of Kamuli Ssabawali, where informal housing, unstable incomes, and limited access to social services define daily life. In this context, tourism development is not the primary policy concern, but it is beginning to register as one pathway toward economic diversification.

What makes Kamuli relevant to the broader discussion of Ugandan tourism infrastructure is precisely its ordinariness. It represents the kind of emerging stopover town that travellers increasingly encounter as they move beyond the national park gateways into the spaces between them. Understanding what facilities exist here, what is improving, and what remains underdeveloped helps visitors plan realistic itineraries and set appropriate expectations.

Accommodation in Kamuli: What Visitors Can Expect

Kamuli's accommodation options fall squarely into the budget and mid-range categories. The town has a number of guesthouses and small hotels that serve the needs of business travellers, government officials on assignment, NGO staff, and the occasional transit visitor. These properties typically offer basic rooms with mosquito nets, en-suite bathrooms with running water (though supply can be intermittent), and simple furnishings. Some provide Wi-Fi, though connection speeds vary widely and should not be relied upon for bandwidth-intensive tasks.

During my visits in January 2026, I observed that most guesthouses in Kamuli were clean and reasonably maintained, with staff who were helpful even if not trained in hospitality to international standards. Room rates were low compared to accommodation near national parks or in Kampala — a reflection of the local market's purchasing power rather than any deliberate positioning for budget tourism. For travellers accustomed to the amenities found at lodges in Bwindi or Queen Elizabeth, the adjustment is significant. There are no swimming pools, no bush-view terraces, no curated wildlife experiences at the doorstep. What Kamuli's guesthouses offer instead is an honest look at how ordinary Ugandans travel and where they stay.

The construction practices behind much of Kamuli's accommodation reflect broader patterns in the municipality. Building is largely informal, with minimal involvement of professional builders. Families and small business owners often construct incrementally, using savings from retail trade or remittances to add rooms or floors over time. This means that even newer-looking properties may have inconsistencies in construction quality — unfinished upper floors, improvised plumbing, or rooms that were clearly not designed with ventilation standards in mind. None of this is unsafe in any immediate sense, but it shapes the experience in ways that visitors from countries with strict building codes will notice.

For context, the Uganda Tourism Board has graded and classified a limited number of accommodation facilities nationwide. The grading programme, which assigns star ratings based on inspections of service quality, amenities, and safety standards, covers only a fraction of the country's total accommodation stock. In eastern Uganda, the proportion of graded properties is notably lower than in the central region, where Kampala alone concentrates the majority of classified hotels. Kamuli, to date, does not feature prominently in the graded accommodation lists. This does not mean accommodation is unavailable or unusable — it means that visitors should approach it without the expectation of standardised quality assurance.

Travellers who prefer a higher standard of accommodation while still wanting to explore the Kamuli area have a practical option: basing themselves in Jinja, which offers a much wider range of lodging from riverside resorts to well-established backpacker hostels, and making Kamuli a day trip or a brief overnight stop. The Jinja Nile Resort, located on the banks of the Nile approximately 100 kilometres southwest of Kamuli, provides a level of comfort — with its twelve-hectare garden grounds, swimming pool, and restaurant — that Kamuli's guesthouses cannot yet match. Other options along the Nile near Jinja, including various lodges and campsites at the former Bujagali Falls area, also serve as viable bases for visitors exploring the wider Busoga region.

Transport, Roads, and Accessibility

Getting to Kamuli from Kampala involves a road journey of approximately 180 kilometres. The route follows the well-maintained Kampala-Jinja highway — one of Uganda's busiest and most heavily invested transport corridors — before branching northeast from Jinja toward Kamuli. The Kampala-Jinja section is paved, relatively fast, and dotted with petrol stations, roadside eateries, and rest stops that have improved noticeably in recent years. During my October 2024 trip along this corridor, I noted the continuing investment in road infrastructure that Uganda has made a national priority, with tarmac surfaces, lane markings, and speed bumps at trading centres all reflecting a systematic approach to road development.

Beyond Jinja, the road quality begins to vary. The main route to Kamuli is tarmacked for significant stretches, but sections remain in varying states of repair. During the rainy season, flat-lying areas around Kamuli are susceptible to flooding, which can disrupt travel and make unpaved feeder roads temporarily impassable. This is a consistent challenge across many Ugandan municipalities that sit in low-lying terrain, and it is something that any visitor planning travel between October and December or March and May should take into account.

Local transport within Kamuli relies on the same systems that serve most Ugandan towns: boda-boda motorcycle taxis, shared matatu minibuses, and a limited number of private hire vehicles. Boda-bodas are ubiquitous and affordable, though they come with well-documented safety risks — including the absence of helmets for passengers, aggressive riding styles, and a lack of insurance. For short distances within the town, they remain the most practical option. Matatus connect Kamuli to Jinja, Iganga, and other towns in the region, operating on informal schedules that depend more on passenger numbers than fixed timetables.

For self-driving visitors, Kamuli presents few difficulties beyond the general challenges of navigating Ugandan roads: unpredictable speed bumps, pedestrians and livestock sharing the carriageway, and the occasional pothole that appears without warning. Fuel is available in the town, and mobile phone coverage from the major Ugandan networks (MTN and Airtel) is generally reliable along the main road corridors. The broader context for Kamuli's transport accessibility is Uganda's ongoing investment in road infrastructure across the country. The Uganda National Roads Authority has been systematically extending and upgrading tarmac roads to connect districts that were previously reachable only by unpaved routes. While Kamuli is not a priority destination in the tourism-focused road-building programme — which has concentrated on routes to national parks and international borders — it benefits from the general improvement of eastern Uganda's road network. The improved connection between Jinja and the northeast means that Kamuli is no longer the isolated town it was a decade ago.

The Service Economy and Emerging Tourism Potential

Kamuli's economy is a microcosm of Uganda's broader transition from a predominantly agricultural base to a more diversified structure that includes trade, transport, and services. Households in Kamuli Municipal Council derive their livelihoods from a mix of petty trade, transport services, informal small-scale manufacturing, and subsistence urban farming. The tertiary sector — encompassing commerce, financial services, education, and hospitality — is the fastest-growing segment of the local economy, though it starts from a low base compared to major urban centres.

Tourism, as a distinct economic activity, occupies a marginal position within this service economy. There are no tour operators based in Kamuli, no booking offices for safari excursions, and no dedicated tourism information centre. The hospitality infrastructure that does exist — guesthouses, small restaurants, bars — serves the local and regional market rather than international visitors. Yet this is precisely the stage at which many of Uganda's now-established tourism towns found themselves a generation ago. Jinja, before the adventure tourism boom of the 2000s, was primarily a commercial town known for its sugar and textile industries. The lesson is that tourism potential can emerge from unexpected places when the right combination of accessibility, attractions, and entrepreneurial initiative aligns.

What Kamuli does have is proximity to the Nile. The western boundary of the district touches the river, and while this stretch of the Nile does not have the dramatic rapids found near Jinja, it offers a quieter, less developed riverside landscape that could appeal to visitors seeking authenticity over adrenaline. Community-based tourism initiatives, which have proven successful in other parts of Uganda — from the Bigodi Wetland Sanctuary near Kibale National Park to various cultural encounters around Bwindi — could theoretically find a footing in Kamuli's riverside communities. However, no such programmes have yet been established in any organised way.

The services sector in Uganda as a whole has been a significant driver of economic growth and employment. Tourism specifically creates jobs across a range of skill levels, from guides and lodge staff to drivers, cooks, and craft sellers. The sector also generates revenue that supports conservation efforts and community development. For Kamuli, the question is not whether tourism could contribute to the local economy — it clearly could — but whether the investment required to develop tourism-ready facilities and services will materialize in a municipality where more immediate development priorities compete for limited resources.

During my January 2026 visit, I spoke with several local business owners who were aware of the tourism potential but lacked both the capital and the knowledge to develop tourism-oriented services. A restaurant owner near the main market mentioned that he occasionally saw foreign visitors passing through but had no idea how to attract more of them or what they might need. This gap between awareness and capacity is one of the defining challenges for tourism development in towns like Kamuli — and it is a gap that organisations such as the Uganda Tourism Board and international development partners could help to bridge.

Practical Considerations for Visitors

For travellers considering a stop in Kamuli, whether as part of a broader eastern Uganda itinerary or as a deliberate exploration of off-circuit Uganda, there are several practical points worth noting. First, accommodation should be booked with realistic expectations. The guesthouses are adequate for a night or two but do not offer the amenities or service levels found at lodges in national park areas. Bringing your own toiletries, a headlamp or torch (power outages occur), and a universal plug adapter is advisable.

Food and drink are readily available. Kamuli has a functioning market where fresh produce — bananas, tomatoes, cassava, beans, and seasonal fruits — can be purchased. Restaurants in the town serve Ugandan staples: posho (maize meal), beans, matoke (steamed plantain), and grilled meat or fish. The quality is generally good, prices are very low by international standards, and the experience offers genuine immersion into the local food culture. Bottled water is available at shops throughout the town.

Healthcare facilities exist in Kamuli, but they are basic. For any serious medical issue, Jinja's hospitals are the nearest facilities with reasonable capacity. Travel insurance that covers medical evacuation is strongly recommended for anyone travelling in eastern Uganda beyond the main cities. Pharmacies in Kamuli stock basic medications, including antimalarials and rehydration salts, but availability of specific drugs cannot be guaranteed.

Currency exchange is not available in Kamuli in the way it is in Kampala or Jinja. Visitors should arrive with sufficient Ugandan shillings for their stay. Mobile money (MTN Mobile Money and Airtel Money) is widely used throughout Uganda and is accepted by many small businesses in Kamuli. ATMs may be available at bank branches in the town centre, but relying on them as the sole source of cash is not advisable.

Security is not a significant concern. Kamuli is a functioning commercial town with regular police presence and no history of targeted incidents against visitors. The general advice for travel in Uganda applies: keep valuables out of sight, avoid displaying large amounts of cash, and exercise the same situational awareness you would in any unfamiliar town. Residents are overwhelmingly friendly and curious about foreign visitors, and interactions are typically positive and welcoming.

The broader lesson from visiting Kamuli is that Uganda's tourism infrastructure is not confined to the glossy lodges and well-trodden paths of the national park circuits. There is a parallel Uganda — of market towns, transit corridors, and emerging service centres — that receives almost no attention from travel media but that shapes the actual experience of moving through the country. Understanding what towns like Kamuli offer, and what they do not, makes for a more informed and ultimately more rewarding journey.

Frequently Asked Questions About Kamuli Tourism Facilities

Are there tourist-grade hotels in Kamuli?

Kamuli offers a range of budget and mid-range guesthouses and hotels, but the town does not yet have internationally graded or luxury accommodation. Most properties serve business travellers, government officials, and NGO workers rather than leisure tourists. Rooms are generally clean and functional, with amenities such as mosquito nets, basic en-suite bathrooms, and sometimes Wi-Fi. Travellers seeking higher-end lodges will find better options in Jinja, which is approximately 100 kilometres to the southwest along the Kampala-Jinja highway.

How do you get to Kamuli from Kampala or Jinja?

Kamuli is reached by road from Kampala via the Kampala-Jinja highway, continuing northeast through Jinja. The total distance from Kampala is roughly 180 kilometres, and the journey takes approximately three to four hours depending on traffic through the Jinja corridor. From Jinja, Kamuli is about 100 kilometres northeast. Public minibus taxis and shared matatus operate regular services along this route. Private hire vehicles and boda-boda motorcycle taxis are also widely available for local transport within the district.

What is there to see and do in Kamuli District?

Kamuli District is not a conventional safari destination, but it offers authentic experiences for travellers interested in eastern Ugandan culture, daily life, and emerging tourism. Points of interest include the Nile river frontage in the western part of the district, local markets that provide genuine immersion into Busoga trading culture, and community-based initiatives that are gradually opening to visitors. The area is also a transit corridor for travellers heading to more established destinations in the northeast.

Is Kamuli safe for international visitors?

Kamuli is generally safe for visitors exercising standard travel precautions. The town is a functioning municipal centre with police presence, health facilities, and a regular commercial economy. During three visits between October 2024 and January 2026, the author experienced no security incidents. As with any Ugandan town outside the major tourism circuits, visitors should take normal precautions regarding personal belongings and avoid walking alone in unfamiliar areas after dark. Residents are typically welcoming and curious about foreign visitors.

What role does tourism play in Kamuli's economy?

Tourism is a minor but growing contributor to Kamuli's economy. The local economy is primarily driven by trade, transport, informal small-scale manufacturing, and subsistence urban farming. The services sector, which includes hospitality and tourism, forms part of the broader tertiary economy. Kamuli Municipal Council's poverty rate of approximately 22 percent — above the national average of 16.9 percent — reflects the economic challenges that limit private investment in tourism infrastructure. However, improved road connections and growing interest in off-the-beaten-path travel could gradually increase tourist traffic through the area.
